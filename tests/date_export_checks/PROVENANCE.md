# DATE export fixtures

These hand-authored fixtures exercise R11–R12/R14/R16 through the public native
definition, export, import and bounded recurrence APIs. Ordinary replay is
offline. No expected value was generated by roc-time or copied from a successful
run. The reference is RFC 5545 (September 2009), specifically
[§3.3.10](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5545.html#section-3.3.10) and
[§3.8.5.3](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5545.html#section-3.8.5.3).
Only factual calendar dates and independently written inputs are reproduced;
no RFC implementation code is adapted.

`wkst-mo` and `wkst-su` use the August 1997 Tuesday/Sunday fortnightly examples
from §3.8.5.3. Their expected dates are the source's dates projected to DATE;
this fixture does not test the example's clock or timezone. `last-weekday`
uses that section's September–November 1997 last-weekday example. Its shuffled
duplicate input is our extension of the same selection, testing canonical
deduplication. The literal outputs remain September 30, October 31 and
November 28.

`monthly` independently applies §3.3.10 invalid-date skipping and COUNT to
January 31, March 31 and May 31, 2025. Recurrence-set union/exclusion semantics
then add February 4 and remove March 31 (even though it is also included).
An exclusion does not extend the counted series into July. `until` checks the
inclusive three-day cutoff; `forever` checks an unbounded definition evaluated
over a three-day half-open window. These are manually derived extensions of
the stated RFC rules, not published RFC example outputs.

Additional manually derived selector fixtures cover positive and negative
values. In 2018, January 1 and 8 are the first two Mondays; unnumbered MO
includes them regardless of the accompanying first/53rd/negative-53rd weekday
selectors. The mixed ordinal input specifically checks the exporter's signed
weekday ordering, including weekdays with no 53rd occurrence that year.
In 2020, the Wednesdays in ISO weeks 1 and 53 (the last week) are January 1
and December 30. For 2025, first/last year-day gives January 1/December 31;
first/last month-day restricted to January/December gives January 1/31 and
December 1/31. First/last Monday within January and February 2025 gives
January 6/27 and February 3/24. These use ordinary Gregorian calendar facts
and RFC signed-selector semantics, independently of production calculations.
COUNT 2147483647 retains the first three days inside a three-day query window;
the large count must not force full-series enumeration.

Canonical bytes are a roc-time profile contract, **not** an RFC claim that
there is one unique property ordering: FREQ, INTERVAL, termination, selectors
in BYMONTH/BYWEEKNO/BYYEARDAY/BYMONTHDAY/BYDAY/BYSETPOS order, then WKST.
INTERVAL and WKST are explicit. Integer selectors are increasing and unique;
BYDAY orders weekdays Monday through Sunday, then ordinals. Exceptions are
sorted unique individual DATE strings. These expected strings were written
from that declared contract independently of the exporter.

Negative fixtures exercise year 0 and 10000 in each date-bearing position,
COUNT 2147483648, disputed native YEARLY defaults, bare BYSETPOS and the 65536
aggregate byte cap. A valid native UNTIL in year zero also needs an anchor
outside the RFC range, so DTSTART rejection takes precedence there; year 10000
isolates UNTIL rejection. INTERVAL 2147483648 is already rejected by the native
constructor, before export. The size fixture supplies 4096 distinct dates in
each exception list: 65536 date bytes alone leave no room for start/rule.
The exact-cap fixture instead uses INTERVAL 1234, whose canonical start/rule
occupy 40 bytes, plus 4096 inclusion and 4091 exclusion DATE entries. Thus
40 + (4096 + 4091) × 8 = 65536 bytes must export and reimport successfully;
one additional exclusion gives 65544 bytes and must fail. Its expected summary
is derived from this arithmetic, avoiding a large repetitive text fixture.

The corpus hash in `scripts/test_date_export.py` pins reviewed expectations.
Changes require reviewing this derivation; replay never refreshes expectations.
The same comparator checks interpreter smoke and dev/speed binaries. Wrong
dates, missing results and malformed JSON are deliberate failure controls.
These finite cases do not establish complete RFC support, timing bounds or
allocation behavior; the separate generated/resource gates supply other
evidence.
